Traceability & Governance

Auditing Autonomous Agents



Pramod Sadalage & Premanand Chandrasekaran · ThoughtWorks

This is Topic 4 — the final content topic. In Topic 3 we established that data needs to be trusted — contracts, quality gates, quarantine patterns. But even with perfect data, when agents act autonomously, who's watching? How do you audit what happened? How do you answer a regulator who asks "why did the agent do this?" That's what this topic addresses. The shift from "what happened" to "why it happened" is the fundamental challenge of agentic governance.

The audit gap

Trade finance scenario

Agent processes letter of credit: checks customer KYC, verifies compliance rules, approves $2.4M transaction — all in 30 seconds.

6 months later…

A regulator asks: "Why was this approved?"

Can you answer with full reasoning chains? Or just timestamps and table names?

Traditional audit

"What" happened — which tables were accessed, timestamps, user IDs

Agentic audit

"Why" — which sources did the agent consult, what reasoning chain led to the decision, what alternatives were considered and rejected

The gap between these two is where regulatory risk lives.

The trade finance scenario makes the stakes concrete. DBS Bank built their agentic AI for trade finance, and governance architecture was the key innovation. Imagine: an agent processes a letter of credit — it checks KYC data, verifies the customer isn't on a sanctions list, evaluates credit terms, and approves a $2.4 million transaction. All in 30 seconds. Six months later, a regulator asks: "Why was this approved?" Traditional audit logs can answer "what happened" — which database tables were queried, at what time, by which service account. But they can't answer "why" — why did the agent decide to check the sanctions list before the credit terms? Why did it approve despite a minor discrepancy in the documentation? What alternatives did it consider? The gap between "what" and "why" is where regulatory risk lives. The EU AI Act Article 12 requires exactly this kind of reasoning chain.

Make the regulatory context concrete and urgent. EU AI Act Article 12 is the most specific regulation — it requires high-risk AI systems to log actions with reasoning chains, retained for at least 6 months. The penalties are massive: up to 35 million euros or 7% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher. For context, 7% of global turnover for a major bank could be billions. Three specific requirements from Article 12: First, log actions with reasoning chains — not just "what happened" but "why." Second, retain those logs for at least 6 months — this means your observability infrastructure needs to handle long-term storage. Third, be able to explain "why" — which data sources were consulted, what logic was applied, what alternatives were considered and rejected. This isn't just an EU issue. The US has the NIST AI Risk Management Framework. The UK has its AI Safety Institute. Singapore has its Model AI Governance Framework. The direction is clear globally: if you deploy autonomous AI systems, you will need to explain what they did and why.

The analogy

Like onboarding a new employee. You don't give them the CEO's email password on day one.

New hire starts with supervision, earns autonomy as trust builds. Same principle for agents.

Each stage requires

• More trust — proven reliability at the previous level

• More observability — increasing monitoring scope

• More governance — stronger audit trails and controls

Staged autonomy is the practical governance framework. You don't deploy an agent with full autonomy on day one — just like you don't give a new employee unrestricted access. Stage 1: Shadow mode — the agent recommends actions but doesn't execute them. A human makes the final decision. The agent's recommendations are fully logged, so you can track accuracy over time. This is the lowest-risk starting point. Stage 2: Supervised — the agent prepares the action but waits for human approval before executing. The human reviews and clicks "approve" or "reject." Full audit trail of both the agent's proposal and the human's decision. Stage 3: Autonomous with guardrails — the agent acts within defined boundaries. Transactions under $10K might be auto-approved, but anything above triggers a human review. Alerts fire on exceptions. Stage 4: Full autonomy — the agent acts independently with continuous monitoring and periodic spot-check audits. This is earned — you get here only after the agent has proven reliable at each previous stage. The analogy resonates with any manager: you wouldn't give a new hire the corporate credit card on day one. They start with purchase requests, graduate to supervised spending, then eventually earn a card with limits. Agents should follow the same trust-building path.

↓

Least privilege

Minimum access needed for the specific task — nothing more

Two critical security patterns for agentic systems. First: delegated access. When Alice asks the agent to check her account, the agent should act with Alice's permissions — not a broad service account that can see all customer data. If the agent uses a shared service account, you lose attribution. When the regulator asks "who accessed this customer's data?", the answer is "the service account" — which tells you nothing. With delegated access, the answer is "Alice's agent, acting on Alice's behalf, with Alice's permissions." Second: just-in-time credentials. Instead of giving the agent a persistent API key that never expires, you issue a short-lived token for each specific task. The agent needs to check the sanctions list? Issue a token that grants OFAC API read access, scoped to the specific customer, valid for 5 minutes. When the task completes, the token expires. No persistent credentials sitting around waiting to be compromised. Third: least privilege. The agent gets the minimum access needed for the specific task. Processing a letter of credit doesn't need access to HR systems or marketing data. These three patterns together solve the attribution and scope problems that plague most current agentic deployments.

Staged autonomy ensures appropriate human oversight — the agents didn't start with full autonomy, they earned it through proven reliability. And critically, governance was built in from day one. It wasn't retrofitted after a compliance incident. It was designed as a core part of the architecture from the start.

Four concrete steps they can take immediately. Step 1: Instrument from day one — the single most important action. Adding observability after deployment is dramatically harder than building it in. Every agent workflow should emit traces with spans for each step, including the reasoning and data sources consulted. Use Langfuse, Arize, or OpenTelemetry for AI — the tooling is mature enough for production. Step 2: Start in shadow mode — the lowest-risk, highest-learning starting point. Deploy agents that recommend actions but don't execute them. A human reviews and decides. This builds the audit trail before you need it for compliance, and it lets you measure agent accuracy before granting autonomy. Step 3: Implement delegated access — agents should inherit the invoking user's permissions, not use broad service accounts. Issue just-in-time credentials for every task with short expiry windows. No persistent tokens sitting around waiting to be compromised. Step 4: Build for the regulator — even if you're not in a regulated industry today, designing your audit trail to answer "why" makes your systems more debuggable, more trustworthy, and more defensible. When regulations arrive — and they will — you'll be ready instead of scrambling.
